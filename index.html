Under the hood

What you don't see, and why it matters

Two websites can look identical in a screenshot and behave completely differently for your customers. Most of what separates a good site from a bad one is invisible, right up until it costs you a customer.

Nine common problems hiding in many websites, and what to watch out for:

  • Problem: Page weight

    Dozens of files before a single word appears

    Template and page-builder sites often load dozens of stylesheets, scripts and several font families just to show the home page, most of it for features the page doesn't even use.

    Why it matters: on a phone with a so-so signal, visitors wait. Many leave before your page finishes loading, and Google factors page speed into search rankings, so a slow site hurts your SEO.

  • Problem: Accessibility

    Barriers you'd never notice with a mouse

    Buttons that people who navigate by keyboard instead of a mouse can't reach. Forms missing the labels screen readers read aloud to people who can't see the screen. Low-contrast text that's hard to read with low vision. Menus a screen reader can't even open. WebAIM's 2026 analysis (opens in a new tab) found detectable accessibility failures on roughly 96% of the top one million home pages.

    Why it matters: about one in four U.S. adults has a disability (opens in a new tab). Those are customers you're turning away. It's also a legal issue: thousands of ADA website lawsuits are filed every year.

  • Problem: Quick fixes

    The "one line of code" accessibility widget

    Overlay plugins promise instant compliance. They paint over problems without fixing the code underneath, and they can make sites harder to use for the people they claim to help.

    Why it matters: you pay a monthly fee and still carry the risk. I diagnose and fix the actual problems instead.

  • Problem: Mobile layout

    Built twice, tested at two sizes

    Many builders hide the desktop version on phones and show a separate copy of the content instead. Both copies still load, and the layout is only checked at a couple of screen sizes.

    Why it matters: real visitors use tablets, folding phones, landscape mode and larger text settings in their browsers. In-between sizes are where layouts quietly break. I build responsive layouts that work at any screen width and test every width and text size. On some CMS platforms, layout control is more limited unless the theme is custom-coded.

  • Problem: Search

    Google reads the code, not the design

    Messy code, headings picked for looks instead of meaningful (semantic) structure, and image descriptions stuffed with keywords to try to game search results.

    Why it matters: clean, honest structure and organic SEO are what search engines and screen readers both rely on. Keyword stuffing can actually hurt your rankings, and it makes your site worse for blind visitors.

  • Problem: Upkeep

    Plugin piles and lock‑in

    Every plugin needs updates, adds weight, and is one more door for hackers. Plugins also fall out of date or get abandoned, and because they lean on each other, updating one can break another. Sites end up frozen like a house of cards, too risky to touch. It's like an old app that stops working after your phone or computer updates, except here it can take part of your site down with it. Sites built in proprietary page builders are also hard to move if you ever change developers.

    Why it matters: lower monthly costs, fewer surprises and a site you actually own. When a CMS is the right call, I set it up lean, and I'm upfront about what that platform can and can't do.

  • Problem: Ownership

    A website you can't take with you

    All-in-one builders host your site on their own servers, and some don't let you move it. Wix, for example, says its sites have to run on Wix's own servers and can't use another host (Wix Help Center (opens in a new tab)), so leaving means rebuilding from scratch. You can keep your web address with another company and point it at a Wix site, but the site itself can't move. Your web address has its own risk: some designers and agencies register a client's domain under their own account, then won't hand it over when the client leaves.

    Why it matters: you can end up paying for a platform you've outgrown, or starting over under a new web address and rebranding. Register your domain in your own name, keep the logins, and find out before you build whether you can take the site with you. Read more in the FAQ

  • Problem: Robo-audits

    The automated "audit" report

    Many marketing agencies and visual designers send website "audits" that are really just an automated crawler report or a quick glance, used to open a sale. Automated tools catch only a fraction of accessibility problems, and none of them can tell you how your site actually works for the people using it.

    Why it matters: a tidy report can miss broken menus, outdated plugins, code errors and barriers you can't see on the surface. A real audit combines several tools with hands-on testing using a keyboard and real devices, plus a look at the code underneath.

  • Problem: Effects

    Motion that costs more than it's worth

    Animation and carousels can be great. The trouble is how most templates do them: auto-playing background videos, sliders that load every image up front, parallax backgrounds that slide at a different speed than the page as you scroll, and effects on every element, all downloaded before visitors see a thing.

    Why it matters: on a cell connection or a limited data plan, that means slow pages and burned data, and constant motion, parallax scrolling especially, can make some people dizzy or motion-sick, so they leave. I use animation where it helps your message and build it light: media loads only when it's needed, carousels stay in the visitor's control, and motion switches off for anyone whose device asks for less of it or is saving data. The animations on this page work exactly that way.

The SEO myth that costs you rankings

More keywords don't automatically mean better rankings. Stuffing your pages and image descriptions with repetitive search terms, or adding hidden keywords that visitors never see, won't give you an edge. Google stopped using the hidden "meta keywords" tag back in 2009, and keyword stuffing violates its spam policies (Google Search Central (opens in a new tab), Google's spam policies (opens in a new tab)).

Good organic SEO starts with helping both people and search engines understand your site. That means clear page structure, descriptive titles, relevant content, useful image descriptions, fast load times, and a solid technical foundation. I build these fundamentals into every site I create, giving search engines a clearer picture of what you offer and visitors a better experience when they find you.

No keyword tricks or ranking promises, just a website built on sound SEO practices from the start.

Accessibility

Accessibility

Accessibility isn't a nice-to-have. It's a legal issue.

It's not only a government issue. Everyday businesses and nonprofits get caught in the net too, usually in one of three ways. The standard that keeps coming up is WCAG 2.1 AA: the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, the international standard for accessible websites. AA is the level most laws and courts point to.

  • You're open to the public. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) covers businesses open to the public, and the U.S. Department of Justice says that includes the goods and services they offer on the web (ADA.gov (opens in a new tab)). More than 3,000 ADA website lawsuits were filed in federal court in 2025, up 27% from the year before (Seyfarth Shaw (opens in a new tab)). Small businesses and online stores are frequent targets.
  • You build or run something for a state or local government. A 2024 federal rule requires state and local government websites and apps to meet WCAG 2.1 AA, and that includes content provided by contractors and outside partners, like a county web page built by a private design firm. Deadlines begin in April 2027 (ADA.gov (opens in a new tab)).
  • You receive federal funding. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act bars disability discrimination by any organization that receives federal financial assistance. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services now spells it out for the organizations it funds, such as clinics and social service nonprofits: their websites and apps must meet WCAG 2.1 AA by May 2027, or May 2028 for those with fewer than 15 employees (Federal Register (opens in a new tab)).
  • Accessibility overlay widgets don't prevent lawsuits. Sites using them were named in more than 1,400 lawsuits in 2025 (Silktide (opens in a new tab), Overlay Fact Sheet (opens in a new tab)).
  • Fixing problems before launch costs far less than responding to a claim and rebuilding afterward.
  • No guarantees. Careful design, building and testing lower the risk, but no one, including me, can promise that a website will never face a complaint or lawsuit.

If you've already received a demand letter, talk to an attorney first.

How I build

The test pattern

A website can look finished and still have real problems underneath. Speed, search, accessibility and how it behaves on different devices are easy to overlook, because the person who built it often can't see them.

I don't treat those as extras or assume the platform handled them. I test them, find the problems and fix them as part of the build.

  • Accessibility: WCAG 2.2, checked with independent tools and by hand.
  • Performance: sized and compressed images, minimal blocking code, tested on real devices.
  • Search basics: semantic HTML, descriptive metadata, link previews, sitemap and robots.
  • Every screen: layouts that re-flow for phones, tablets and desktops instead of shrinking.

Modern tools, old-school standards

Like most developers today, I use AI-assisted tools to draft code faster, especially JavaScript. Then I review, test and adjust everything myself. Less time typing means more time testing, and a real person stays accountable for what ships.

Automated scores are a floor, not a finish line. They catch about a third of accessibility problems. The rest is careful manual work.

About

From wrenches to websites

Hi, I'm Matt. Before web development, I spent close to two decades in the automotive world, including more than 15 years as a technician.

Modern cars are computers on wheels. Most people still picture grease and guesswork, but the real work was diagnostics: reading live sensor data, tracing wiring diagrams, reprogramming control modules and working safely on high-voltage hybrid and electric systems. A trouble code doesn't tell you what to replace; it tells you where to start looking. An oxygen (O2) sensor reading lean usually isn't a bad sensor. It's often doing its job, reporting a vacuum leak or a fuel injector problem. A parts changer swaps the sensor and guesses again. A real diagnostician tests until the cause is proven.

A dead battery might come down to a parasitic drain from a module that never goes to sleep, or a drivability problem that no amount of swapping parts under the hood will fix, because only a software update solves it. Guessing gets expensive fast, so you learn to reproduce the problem, prove the cause, repair it properly and confirm the fix before the car goes back to the customer. The last thing you want is a comeback: the same car, back with the same problem, costing the customer more time and money and costing you their trust.

  1. Test
  2. Find the real cause
  3. Fix it properly
  4. Prove it works

That's exactly how I work on websites. An automated scan is like a trouble code: a starting point, not a diagnosis. Reproduce the problem, find the real cause, fix it instead of patching over it, and prove it works. I've since built and supported websites for more than a dozen nonprofits at a web agency, led a remote web team for a global nonprofit, and run my own small online business, so I know what owners are juggling.

Client empowerment, not gatekeeping. As a service writer and technician, I found that one of the things customers valued most was being able to understand what was happening with their vehicles and why. I bring that same approach to web development. I believe in explaining the work, sharing knowledge, and making sure you understand how to manage your own website. My job isn't to make myself indispensable by keeping you in the dark. It's to build something that works and give you the confidence and tools to take it from there.

I'm based in the Greater Seattle area and happy to work remotely.

Does a custom site cost more than a template or website builder?

Up front, often yes. But a template's default design only covers the look. Out of the box, most templates aren't tuned for speed, accessibility, search or best practices. That work still has to be designed and built in, whichever platform you use. The visuals are one piece of the puzzle. What matters just as much is how browsers, screen readers and search engines read the code underneath.

Think of a resume with a beautiful layout that the automated HR hiring software can't read or parse. It looks great, but it may never reach a real person for review if it's not designed to be machine friendly and readable. A website can look good to you and still turn customers away, and not show up when people search for what you offer.

There's also the long-term cost to consider. Website builders typically charge ongoing subscription fees, and extra features can mean additional monthly costs. Depending on your needs, a custom-built site can be a better investment over time, with fewer recurring platform costs and more control over what you own.

That said, custom development isn't the right fit for everyone. If a website builder makes more sense for your budget and goals, I'll tell you. And if you already have a site you're happy with, I can help improve its speed, accessibility, and overall performance without starting from scratch.

Why start with an audit before you fix or change my site?

Because a quick fix on a site nobody has looked at can break something bigger. Websites are a lot like modern cars: one change can affect systems you didn't expect. A plugin update can break a checkout page, a theme edit can knock out your contact form, and changing page addresses can undo years of search rankings.

An audit is the diagnosis before the repair. I find out how your site is built, what it depends on (plugins, theme, custom code and hosting), whether it has working backups, and where the risks are. Then you get a clear plan and a quote you can count on, instead of surprises halfway through.

It's the same reason a good shop won't start replacing parts before it knows what's wrong. Guessing is how small jobs turn into expensive ones. For a small, clearly defined change, a $100 starter review may be all you need.

Questions to ask any web developer before you hire

  • Will you test my site by hand, not just with automated tools? That includes getting around with only the keyboard (the Tab key) and checking the labels screen readers read aloud.
  • What are the Lighthouse scores on a phone and on a desktop, and can I see them? (They can differ a lot.)
  • If my site uses a CMS, how many plugins will it need, and who keeps them updated?
  • Will the layout work at every screen size, including tablets, or only phone and desktop?
  • Do you use an accessibility overlay? (The right answer is no.)
  • If we part ways, can I take my site with me?
