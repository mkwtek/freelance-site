Under the hood

What you don't see, and why it matters

Two websites can look identical in a screenshot and behave completely differently for your customers. Most of what separates a good site from a bad one is invisible, right up until it costs you a customer.

  • Page weight

    Dozens of files before a single word appears

    Template and page-builder sites often load dozens of stylesheets, scripts and several font families just to show the home page, most of it for features the page doesn't even use.

    Why it matters: on a phone with a so-so signal, visitors wait. Many leave before your page finishes loading, and Google factors page speed into search rankings.

  • Accessibility

    Barriers you'd never notice with a mouse

    Buttons a keyboard can't reach, forms without labels, low-contrast text, menus a screen reader can't open. WebAIM's 2026 analysis found detectable accessibility failures on roughly 96% of home pages.

    Why it matters: about one in four U.S. adults has a disability. Those are customers you're turning away. It's also a legal issue: thousands of ADA website lawsuits are filed every year, mostly against small businesses.

  • Quick fixes

    The "one line of code" accessibility widget

    Overlay plugins promise instant compliance. They paint over problems without fixing the code underneath, and they can make sites harder to use for the people they claim to help.

    Why it matters: you pay a monthly fee and still carry the risk. I fix the actual problems instead.

  • Mobile layout

    Built twice, tested at two sizes

    Many builders hide the desktop version on phones and show a separate copy of the content instead. Both copies still load, and the layout is only checked at a couple of screen sizes.

    Why it matters: real visitors use tablets, folding phones, landscape mode and larger text settings. In-between sizes are where layouts quietly break. I test every width and text size.

  • Search

    Google reads the code, not the design

    Messy markup, headings chosen for looks instead of structure, and image descriptions stuffed with city names to game search results.

    Why it matters: clean, honest structure is what search engines and screen readers both rely on. Keyword stuffing helps no one and makes your site worse for blind visitors.

  • Upkeep

    Plugin piles and lock-in

    Every plugin needs updates, adds weight, and is one more door for hackers. Sites built in proprietary page builders are also hard to move if you ever change developers.

    Why it matters: lower monthly costs, fewer surprises and a site you actually own. When a CMS is the right call, I set it up lean.

  • Effects

    Motion that costs more than it's worth

    Animation and carousels can be great. The trouble is how most templates do them: auto-playing background videos, sliders that load every image up front, and effects on every element, all downloaded before visitors see a thing.

    Why it matters: on a cell connection or a limited data plan, that means slow pages and burned data, and constant motion can make some people motion-sick. I use animation where it helps your message and build it light: media loads only when it's needed, carousels stay in the visitor's control, and motion switches off for anyone whose device asks for less of it or is saving data. The animations on this page work exactly that way.

Accessibility isn't a nice-to-have. It's a legal issue.

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, businesses open to the public are expected to serve people with disabilities, and courts increasingly apply that to websites. Most owners don't find out until a demand letter arrives.

  • About 4,000 ADA website lawsuits were filed in federal court in 2025 alone, and most targeted small businesses and online stores.
  • New federal rules require state and local government websites to meet WCAG 2.1 AA, including sites built for them by contractors. That matters for nonprofits and vendors who work with public agencies.
  • Accessibility overlay widgets don't prevent lawsuits. Sites using them are still being sued.
  • Fixing problems before launch costs far less than responding to a claim and rebuilding afterward.

I build and test to WCAG 2.1 AA as standard practice. I'm not a lawyer and this isn't legal advice. If you've already received a demand letter, talk to an attorney first.

Questions to ask any web developer before you hire

  • Will you test my site with a keyboard and a screen reader?
  • What are the Lighthouse scores on a phone, and can I see them?
  • How many plugins will my site need, and who keeps them updated?
  • Will the layout work at every screen size, or only phone and desktop?
  • Do you use an accessibility overlay? (The right answer is no.)
  • If we part ways, can I take my site with me?

How I build

The test pattern

A website can look finished and still have real problems underneath. Speed, search, accessibility and how it behaves on different devices are easy to overlook, because the person who built it often can't see them.

I don't treat those as extras or assume the platform handled them. I test them, find the problems and fix them as part of the build.

  • Accessibility: WCAG 2.1, checked with independent tools and by hand.
  • Performance: sized and compressed images, minimal blocking code, tested on real devices.
  • Search basics: semantic HTML, descriptive metadata, link previews, sitemap and robots.
  • Every screen: layouts that re-flow for phones, tablets and desktops instead of shrinking.

Modern tools, old-school standards

I can write HTML and CSS by hand, and did for years. Today I use AI-assisted tools to draft code far faster, especially JavaScript, then review, test and adjust every piece myself. Less time typing means more time testing, and you get modern speed with a real person accountable for every line that ships.

Automated scores are a floor, not a finish line. They catch about a third of accessibility problems. The rest is careful manual work.
