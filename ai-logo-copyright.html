AI art & copyright

Made it with AI? Make it yours.

AI can turn an idea into a logo in seconds, and lots of businesses are using it. What most people don't know is that under current U.S. law, art made entirely by AI can't be copyrighted. Your AI tool's terms may say you own what it makes, but a company's terms can't create a copyright the law doesn't recognize. I'm a graphic designer as well as a web developer, and I can turn your AI mockup into artwork made by a person.

What the law says right now

  • Copyright needs a human author. In March 2025, a federal appeals court ruled that the Copyright Act requires a work to be created by a human being to be registered (Thaler v. Perlmutter, D.C. Circuit (opens in a new tab)). In March 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal, so that ruling stands (SCOTUSblog case page (opens in a new tab)).
  • Prompts aren't enough. The U.S. Copyright Office's January 2025 report says AI output can be protected "only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements," and that writing prompts alone doesn't qualify (Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2 (opens in a new tab)).
  • Human work still counts. The same report says protection can apply where "a human makes creative arrangements or modifications of the output," and that using AI as a tool doesn't rule out copyright on its own (Library of Congress announcement (opens in a new tab)).
  • Only the human part is protected. When you register a work that includes AI material, the Copyright Office asks you to disclose the AI parts and leave them out of your claim (registration guidance, March 2023 (opens in a new tab)). In one well-known case, a comic book called Zarya of the Dawn, the Office registered the author's text and the way she selected and arranged the images, but not the AI-generated images themselves (Copyright Office decision letter (opens in a new tab)).

What that means for your logo

  • If your logo came straight out of an AI tool, someone else could likely copy it, and copyright law may give you little to stop them.
  • Small tweaks may not be enough. The more of the final design that comes from a person's own creative choices, the stronger the case for copyright.
  • Trademark is a separate protection. A logo that identifies your business can be a trademark, which is handled by the USPTO, not the Copyright Office (USPTO: What is a trademark? (opens in a new tab)). Very simple logos, like a name in a standard font or basic shapes, usually can't be copyrighted anyway (Copyright Office Circular 33 (opens in a new tab)), so for a logo, trademark is often the bigger question.

How I can help

  • Redraw it as vector: I rebuild your AI mockup as clean vector artwork. AI images are pixels, and they often come with blurry edges, garbled lettering and odd details. Vector art stays sharp at any size, from a business card to a sign or a shirt.
  • Rework it: I make real creative changes to the design, like the layout, shapes, lettering, colors and details, so the final piece reflects a person's choices, not just a prompt.
  • Start fresh: use your AI images as a mood board, and I'll design an original from your idea.
  • Use it everywhere: logos, brand guides, business cards, flyers, posters, and merch like shirts, hoodies, hats, stickers and bags, with files prepped for the way each one gets printed.
  • Keep the paper trail: you get the final files plus the working files and earlier versions, which show the human work that went into your design.

What I can't promise

I'm not a lawyer, and nothing on this page is legal advice. I can't promise any design will qualify for copyright or trademark protection. That's decided by the Copyright Office, the USPTO and the courts. This area of law is still changing, and more cases are working their way through the courts. If protecting your logo matters to your business, talk to an intellectual property attorney. What I can promise is real design work by a person, done with the current guidance in mind.

This page covers U.S. law. Other countries have their own rules.
